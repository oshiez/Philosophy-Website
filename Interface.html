The Toulmin Model

Toulmin's Model of Argumentation is comprised of six parts you can use to pick apart somebody's argument, or a tool you can use to build your own. It was created by philosopher Stephen Toulmin. Using this model, you can form airtight arguments and pick apart your opponent's argument if you use it the right way.

Claim

A claim is usually labeled as a conclusion you want to get to with a group, audience, or another person.

A simple way to identify a claim would be to ask yourself, "Do they want me to do something, is it a fact, or are they arguing if something is good or bad? Is it a conclusion?"

Claim of Fact

A claim of fact is something that can be true or false, something that exists or doesn't exist, or something that is real or fake.

A simple way to ask if something is a claim of fact would be "Is it true?", "Is it real?", or "Does it exist?"

Claim of Value

A claim of value is something that can be right or wrong, good or bad, beautiful or ugly, or something that is positive or negative. Most importantly, it can be something with morality.

A simple way to ask if something is a claim of value would be "Is it good?", "Is it right?", or "Is it kind?"

Claim of Policy

A claim of policy is a demand of an action that needs to be done to solve a problem. It usually combines a claim of fact and a claim of value together to create a policy that can be argued for people to follow.

A simple way to ask if something is a claim of policy would be "Are they asking something?", "Is this a demand?", or "Do they want me, someone else, or a group to do something?"

An example would be "You should go inside." (Claim of policy declaring the person mentioned as "You" to go inside.)

Warrant

A warrant is a rule or human ethical (usually) standard that connects your grounds to your claim, providing a reason for your claim.

A simple way to identify a warrant would be to ask yourself, "Is this a reason for something, or is it an explanation why something is positive or negative, or right or wrong?".

An example would be "Getting wet is uncomfortable." (Warrant stating that getting wet is uncomfortable. This correctly joins the grounds and claim together and provides a reason to go inside, though in this instance it is obvious or redundant, and in some cases, condescending. Philosophers label things that aren't mentioned because the audience or the other person are assumed to know as common sense as an Enthymeme.)

Grounds

Also called data, grounds are identifiable facts that tie to your claim, and serves an important purpose in proof and rigidity.

A simple way to identify grounds would be to ask yourself, "Is this something I can measure with a tool, or a fact I can identify? Is it something real and meaningful?"

An example would be "It is raining outside." (Grounds stating a fact that it is raining outside. If this is not interpreted/proven as fact, it can be reduced to a claim of fact that can be debated upon to reach a conclusion that this claim is true or false.)

Backing

A backing is a verifiable fact that solidifies your warrant if it is unstable.

A simple way to identify a backing would be to ask yourself, "Is this information that came from somebody important, or something that can be verified and ties to the warrant to solidify it?"

An example would be "X company states that staying in the rain can lower your body temperature leading to a weakened immune system and shivering." (Backing that involves the experts in X company who state the negative effects of rain on the human body physically.)

Qualifier

A qualifier is usually a word that is used to convey how sure the speaker is in their statement or a part or percentage in a demographic or statistic. It is used to protect your thesis/antithesis from being disproven by an exception, or from understatements.

A simple way to identify a qualifier would be to analyze the words being used in the thesis/antithesis. Ask yourself "Are they saying they're absolutely sure about this, partially sure, or barely sure at all? Are they referencing everyone on earth, a nation of people, a small group, or only a few people?"

An example would be "You should go inside." (Qualifier "Should" that clarifies it is only a recommendation and not an absolute rule or necessity.)

Rebuttal

A rebuttal is an exception to your thesis/antithesis. It is something that explains potential caveats or edge cases where your thesis/antithesis does not apply. If your thesis/antithesis does not have any rebuttals, it is likely too rigid and easy to be proven wrong.

A simple way to identify a rebuttal would be to ask yourself, "Are they explaining what is exempt from their claim? Are they using sentences like or similar to 'Unless X, it is Y'? Are they providing things that can be excluded from their points?"

An example would be "Unless you have an umbrella." (A rebuttal stating unless they have an umbrella, they are safe from the rain thus the claim is void.)
