The Toulmin Model

Toulmin's Model of Argumentation was created by philosopher Stephen Toulmin, and is comprised of six parts that you can use to pick apart somebody's argument and a tool you can use to build your own. Using this model, you can form airtight arguments and dismantle your opponent's argument.
The parts include: Claim, Grounds, Warrant, Backing, Qualifier, and Rebuttal.

Here is a clear example:
If you start to feel uncomfortable or develop any health risks due to the rain, you should go inside, unless you have an umbrella. It's raining, and getting wet makes some people uncomfortable. Staying in the rain can lower your body temperature which may cause shivering and also weaken your immune system.

More information about The Toulmin Model is provided below.

Claim

A claim is the conclusion or position the speaker wants the opponent or audience to accept.

A simple way to identify a claim would be to ask yourself, "Do they want me to do something, is it a fact, or are they arguing if something is good or bad? Is it a conclusion?"

Claim of Fact

A claim of fact is something that can be true or false, something that exists or doesn't exist, or something that is real or fake.

A simple way to ask if something is a claim of fact would be "Is it true?", "Is it real?", or "Does it exist?"

Claim of Value

A claim of value is something that can be right or wrong, good or bad, beautiful or ugly, or something that is positive or negative. Most importantly, it can be something with morality.

A simple way to ask if something is a claim of value would be "Is it good?", "Is it right?", or "Is it kind?"

Claim of Policy

A claim of policy is a demand of an action that needs to be done to solve a problem. It usually combines a claim of fact and a claim of value together to create a policy that can be argued for people to follow.

A simple way to ask if something is a claim of policy would be "Are they asking something?", "Is this a demand?", or "Do they want me, someone else, or a group to do something?"

An example would be "You should go inside." (Claim of policy declaring the person mentioned as "You" to go inside.)

Warrant

A warrant is a rule that connects your grounds to your claim, providing a reason for your claim.

A simple way to identify a warrant would be to ask yourself, "Is this a reason for something, or is it an explanation why something is positive or negative, or right or wrong?".

An example would be "If the rain makes you uncomfortable, it's best to go inside." (Warrant stating that getting wet is uncomfortable. This connects the claim and grounds together and provides a reason to go inside, though you wouldnt tell this to someone usually. Philosophers label things that aren't mentioned because the audience or the other person are assumed to know as common sense as an Enthymeme.)

Grounds

Also called data, grounds is a point of evidence or observation that supports your claim.

A simple way to identify grounds would be to ask yourself, "Is this something I can measure with a tool, or a fact I can identify? Is it something real and meaningful?"

An example would be "It is raining outside." (Grounds stating a fact that it is raining outside. If this is not interpreted/proven as fact, it can be reduced to a claim of fact that can be debated upon to reach a conclusion that this claim is true or false.)

Backing

A backing is support that solidifies your warrant if it is unstable.

A simple way to identify a backing would be to ask yourself, "Is this information that came from somebody important, or information that solidifies the warrant?"

An example would be "Staying in the rain can lower your body temperature leading to a weakened immune system and shivering." (Backing that involves the negative effects of rain on the human body.)

Qualifier

A qualifier is usually a word that is used to convey how sure the speaker is in their statement or a part or percentage in a demographic or statistic. It is used to limit your scope or strength of your claim.

A simple way to identify a qualifier would be to analyze the words being used in the claim. Ask yourself "Are they saying they're absolutely sure about this, partially sure, or barely sure at all? Are they referencing everyone on earth, a nation of people, a small group, or only a few people?"

An example would be "You should go inside." (Qualifier "Should" that clarifies it is only a recommendation and not an absolute rule or necessity.)

Rebuttal

A rebuttal is an exception to your claim. It is something that explains potential caveats or edge cases where your claim does not apply. If you never consider any exceptions, it is likely too rigid and easy to be proven wrong.

A simple way to identify a rebuttal would be to ask yourself, "Are they explaining what is exempt from their claim? Are they using sentences like or similar to 'Unless X, it is Y'? Are they providing things that can be excluded from their points?"

An example would be "Unless you have an umbrella." (A rebuttal stating unless they have an umbrella, they are safe from the rain thus the claim is void.)
